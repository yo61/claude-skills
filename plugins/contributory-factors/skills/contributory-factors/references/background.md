# Background: Contributory Factors and the London Protocol

## Origins in Healthcare Safety Science

The contributory factors approach was pioneered in healthcare, drawing on earlier work in aviation, nuclear, and industrial safety. The key intellectual lineage is:

### James Reason's Organisational Accident Model
In the 1990s, James Reason developed the "Swiss cheese" model of accident causation, showing that accidents result from the alignment of multiple failures across different levels of a system — not from a single point of failure. His model distinguished between:

- **Active failures**: Unsafe acts or omissions by those at the "sharp end" (frontline workers) whose actions can have immediate consequences. These include slips, lapses, mistakes, and violations.
- **Latent conditions**: Features of the organisation or system that influence those on the frontline and may increase the likelihood of active failures. These include high workload, inadequate knowledge, poor supervision, stressful environments, rapid organisational change, poor communication systems, and inadequate maintenance.
- **Defences and barriers**: Safeguards designed to protect against hazards — physical barriers, procedural controls, human checks, automated systems, and administrative restrictions.

### From Reason to the London Protocol
Vincent, Taylor-Adams and colleagues at the Clinical Risk Unit, University College London, extended Reason's model for healthcare use. They developed a broad framework of "contributory factors" to replace Reason's more industrial terminology of "error producing conditions" and "latent failures." The term "contributory factors" was deliberately chosen as more neutral and more broadly applicable.

Their work produced:
1. The ALARM/CRU protocol (2000) — the first systematic framework
2. The London Protocol (2004) — a refined, widely-adopted version
3. The London Protocol 2024 — a major update reflecting 20 years of experience

### Why the London Protocol Rejects "Root Cause Analysis"

The 2024 London Protocol explicitly states: "We do not use the term 'root cause analysis'. In practice the term 'root cause analysis' is often used as a generic term to cover any kind of incident investigation, but we regard it as misleading in several respects."

The problems with root cause analysis include:
- It implies there is a single root cause, or at least a small number. In practice, the picture is much more fluid.
- It implies the primary purpose is to look back. The real purpose should be forward-looking: to improve system safety.
- Research has found that RCA often produces formulaic recommendations that are not clearly related to the contributory factors identified, are often weak, and fail to address substantial underlying problems (Peerally et al., 2017; Kellogg et al., 2017).
- RCA can degenerate into a bureaucratic process producing repetitive reports and unachievable recommendations (Vincent et al., 2025).

The London Protocol instead uses "systems analysis" — a comprehensive examination of the interplays between human, technical, and organisational factors.

## The Eight Levels of Contributory Factors (London Protocol 2024)

The 2024 edition expanded the original seven categories to eight, adding a dedicated technology/information systems category:

### 1. Patient Factors (Subject/Client Factors in non-clinical contexts)
In clinical settings: the patient's illness, complexity, co-morbidities, language, communication ability, personality, and social factors. In other contexts: the characteristics, complexity, or state of the subject at the centre of the incident.

### 2. Individual (Staff) Factors
Knowledge and skills, experience, physical and mental health, motivation. The confidence and assurance of staff may be particularly important — risk attaches both to being nervous and unsure, and to being overconfident.

### 3. Task Factors
Task design and clarity of structure, availability and use of protocols, availability and accuracy of test results or information needed to complete the task.

### 4. Team Factors
Verbal communication (between junior and senior, between professions, between departments), written communication (legibility, completeness, availability of records), supervision and seeking help, team structure (skills mix, balance of experience).

### 5. Electronic Information Systems and Technology
Added in the 2024 edition. The design, usability, reliability, and integration of digital tools, devices, software, and information systems. AI and clinical decision support systems increasingly influence care processes.

### 6. Work Environmental Factors
Staffing levels and skills mix, workload and shift patterns, design and availability of equipment, administrative and managerial support, physical layout and conditions.

### 7. Organisational and Management Factors
Financial resources and constraints, organisational structure, policy standards and goals, safety culture and priorities, management decisions about staffing, training, and equipment.

### 8. Institutional Context
Economic and regulatory context, national/industry standards and requirements, external policy environment, broader political and economic climate.

### How the Levels Interact
The framework describes a hierarchy where each level influences those closer to the frontline. Institutional context shapes organisational decisions, which determine the work environment, which affects how teams function, which influences individual performance. But the influence is not simply top-down — factors at every level interact in complex ways.

A key distinction: **specific contributory factors** are isolated conditions unique to a particular incident. **General contributory factors** reflect systemic conditions that could contribute to many incidents. For instance, a one-off miscommunication between two individuals is specific; a culture where junior staff feel unable to challenge senior colleagues is general. General factors are usually more important to address.

## The Yorkshire Contributory Factors Framework (YCFF)

Developed by the Yorkshire Quality and Safety Research Group (Lawton et al., 2012), the YCFF was derived from a systematic review of 83 research studies on the causes of hospital patient safety incidents. It provides an evidence-based framework organised into five concentric domains, from proximal to distal:

1. **Active failures** (centre): Slips, lapses, mistakes, deviations from policy
2. **Situational factors**: Team factors, individual staff factors, task characteristics, patient factors
3. **Local working conditions**: Workload/staffing, leadership/supervision, equipment/supplies
4. **Latent/organisational factors**: Physical environment, inter-departmental support, scheduling, staff training/education
5. **Latent/external factors**: Equipment design, national policies, safety culture, communication

The YCFF research found the five most frequent contributory factors across studies were: active failures (18.2%), individual factors (11%), communication (7.9%), equipment and supplies (6.6%), and management of staff and staffing levels (5.8%).

While developed for clinical practice, the YCFF's evidence-based categorisation reinforces the London Protocol's framework and demonstrates that incidents consistently involve factors at multiple levels.

## The Broader Principle: Systems Thinking

Both frameworks embody a systems-thinking approach that has proven valuable far beyond healthcare:

- **Aviation**: Crew Resource Management and the "dirty dozen" human factors
- **Nuclear**: Defence-in-depth and probabilistic safety assessment
- **Software engineering**: Blameless post-mortems, chaos engineering
- **Manufacturing**: Toyota Production System, Deming's systems approach
- **Organisational psychology**: Normal Accident Theory (Perrow), High Reliability Organisations

The common thread: complex systems fail in complex ways. Seeking a single "root cause" is almost always an oversimplification that leads to ineffective interventions. Understanding the constellation of contributory factors leads to more robust and sustainable improvements.
